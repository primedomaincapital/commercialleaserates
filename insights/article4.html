The Concession Arms Race: What Your Office Lease Really Costs in 2026

TI allowances hit $125–$150/SF in Denver. Free rent stretches to 3 years. Why headline office rents are fiction — and how to price the real deal.

By Prime Domain Capital · October 2026

The asking rent on a Denver office tower says $42 a square foot. The tenant signing the lease is getting $125 to $150 per square foot in tenant improvement allowance, multiple years of free rent, and up to three years of free parking. The $42 number is not a lie, exactly. It is just the least informative number in the negotiation.

Welcome to the concession arms race — the defining feature of the 2026 office market, and the reason comparing headline rents across buildings has become an exercise in fiction.

How we got here

The pandemic emptied offices in 2020. Remote work normalized through 2021 and 2022. Return-to-office mandates created a fractured demand picture through 2023 and 2024. By 2025, the market had settled into an equilibrium where vacancy stayed stubbornly high even as lease signings picked up — and landlords adapted their deal structures accordingly.

The 2019 playbook was base-rent negotiation. The 2026 playbook is package engineering. Per CBRE’s market data, tenant improvement allowances in primary tech markets averaged $65 to $85 per square foot in 2025, up from a pre-pandemic norm of $35 to $55. Free rent stretched from the historical one to two months on a five-year term to three to six months. Newmark reports TI allowances in major gateway markets now sit roughly 75% above pre-pandemic levels.

The Denver numbers show how extreme it has become. TI allowances climbed from roughly $90–$100 per square foot to $125–$150. Some deals include multiple years of free rent. In Chicago’s Fulton Market, Class A tenants command $60 to $80 per square foot on a ten-year lease — about a month of free rent per lease year — while Class B space goes for $32 to $38 gross with far skimpier packages. In Portland, office TI runs $30 to $80-plus per square foot; in Indianapolis, a seven-to-ten-year lease can command $60-plus, while five years buys $10 to $25.

Newmark’s 2026 data captures the paradox: asking rents rose 2.6% year over year, yet effective rents remain under pressure because landlords keep spending aggressively to fill space. Office construction hit a 14-year low — just 13.6 million square feet of completions expected in 2026 — but the pipeline collapse has not yet absorbed the vacancy overhang. Denver sits at 26.6 to 28.7% vacancy. Indianapolis Class A is at 21.5%.

The anatomy of a 2026 package

A competitive office deal in 2026 is assembled from five components, and the base rent is arguably the least important:

Free rent. The standard has moved from one month per lease year to well beyond it in soft markets. On a ten-year lease, that is ten-plus months of abatement — nearly a full year of occupancy at zero rent. Structure matters: front-loaded abatement helps cash flow during the expensive move-in period; amortized abatement smooths the effective rate.

Tenant improvement allowance. The big-ticket item. At $125 per square foot on a 10,000-square-foot lease, the landlord is contributing $1.25 million toward the buildout. But read the work letter: the allowance is usually a reimbursement, not an upfront payment. The tenant hires the contractor, pays the invoices, and gets paid back 30 to 60 days after submitting a complete draw package — often with 10% held back until completion. On a mid-size buildout, that means carrying six figures for months.

Term length as leverage. The single strongest variable. Landlords amortize the allowance through rent, so a longer commitment buys a bigger contribution. The Indianapolis data is typical: five years buys $10–$25 per square foot; seven to ten years buys $60-plus. Every additional year of term is worth real dollars in TI — which is why landlords push term so hard.

Parking and operating expense caps. Three years of free parking in Denver is not a throwaway — at $200 a month per stall for 40 employees, that is $288,000. Base-year stops on operating expenses, caps on controllable expenses, and audit rights all move the effective economics more than a dollar on the base rent.

The hidden economics. Here is what the headline rent obscures: on a ten-year, 10,000-square-foot lease at $42 gross with $125/SF TI and 12 months of free rent, the landlord’s total concession package is roughly $2.3 million against $4.2 million in gross rent. The effective rent — the actual economics — is closer to $23 per square foot. The $42 number exists largely to protect the building’s appraised value and the landlord’s financing covenants.

The counterargument: why landlords do it this way

It looks irrational — why not just cut the rent to $23 and skip the theater? Because commercial real estate is valued on net operating income divided by a cap rate, and headline rents drive appraised values, loan covenants, and the pricing of neighboring deals. A building full of tenants at $42 face rent with heavy concessions appraises far better than the same building at $23 straight rent. The landlord’s lender, the landlord’s investors, and the landlord’s next buyer all prefer the fiction.

There is also a selection effect. Generous TI packages attract tenants who invest in the space — the kind who sign ten-year leases and become sticky. A straight rent cut attracts nobody in particular. The concession package is, among other things, a screening mechanism.

And in genuinely tight submarkets, the arms race does not apply. Fishers, Indiana sits at 6.6% vacancy — tenants there have little leverage. In Ashburn-style zero-vacancy pockets, there are no concessions at all. The national averages describe a bifurcated market, not a uniform one.

How to price the real deal

Stop comparing asking rents. The only number that matters is the effective rent: total gross rent over the term, minus the amortized value of every concession, divided by square footage and term years. Get three proposals on identical assumptions — same term, same TI scope, same base year — and model the effective rate for each.

Negotiate the package, not the rent. The landlord would rather give you $20 more per square foot in TI than cut the face rent by $2 — it protects their valuation while costing them roughly the same in present value. Use that preference. Push on free rent timing (front-loaded beats amortized), TI disbursement (progress payments beat completion-only), and the work letter (design fees and soft costs included beats hard-costs-only).

And remember the meta-lesson of 2026: in a market where the headline number is engineered, the sophisticated tenant prices the package. The naive one negotiates the rent.
