18.2%: The Office Market’s Long Climb Back

U.S. office vacancy fell to 18.2% in Q3 2026, the fifth straight quarterly decline, with leasing on pace to beat the 2022 record. Prime vacancy in Midtown Manhattan is 1.9%. The market split in two.

By Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

A leasing broker in Midtown Manhattan took a call in October 2026 that would have been unthinkable three years earlier. A tenant wanted prime space, and the broker had a waiting list. Not a shortlist. A waiting list. In the same week, a broker in Denver was offering $125 to $150 per square foot in tenant improvement allowance, multiple years of free rent, and three years of free parking to fill a tower at 26% vacancy. Both brokers work in the same national office market. They no longer work in the same business.

The numbers that separate them arrived in CBRE’s third-quarter 2026 report. National office vacancy fell to 18.2%, the fifth straight quarterly decline, down 60 basis points from a year earlier. Leasing reached 63.8 million square feet in the quarter, up 4% year over year, and CBRE says full-year volume is on pace to beat the 2022 record of 238 million square feet. Net absorption was positive for the tenth quarter in a row, at 4.9 million square feet. Average asking rent rose 3.1% from a year earlier to $37.95 per square foot, the fastest increase since 2016 and well above the 30-year average of about 2%. New leases, as opposed to renewals, made up 61% of leasing so far this year, up from 56% in 2025.

The prime split

The recovery is not evenly spread, and the averages hide the story. CBRE puts prime office vacancy at 11.9%, down 250 basis points in a year, and prime vacancy in Midtown Manhattan at just 1.9%. The flight to quality that defined the last three years has become a stampede: tenants want the best buildings in the best locations, and there are not enough of them. Office construction hit a 14-year low, with just 13.6 million square feet of completions expected in 2026, so the shortage of prime space is structural, not cyclical.

The demand side has a new driver. CBRE’s occupier survey found 89% of employers now require at least three days a week in the office, up from 78% a year earlier. Return-to-office mandates, once treated as a temporary post-pandemic experiment, are now the operating assumption, and they are converting directly into leasing: tenants who spent three years shrinking their footprints are now signing new leases for space they actually intend to use. Piedmont Office Realty Trust reported cash leasing spreads of 26.2%, one of the highest in its history, and record net effective rents of $29.31 per square foot.

The geography of the recovery is uneven in instructive ways. Palm Beach County recorded 39,000 square feet of net absorption in the third quarter, with overall vacancy at 14.6% and Class A direct vacancy at 12.4%. Fiserv signed a 254,000-square-foot lease in North Boca, the largest office lease in South Florida since 2020. West Palm Beach Class A asking rents reached $46.66 per square foot, up 45.6% from $32.04 in the third quarter of 2021. Boston, by contrast, saw vacancy fall to 18.5% but average asking rents decline $2.63 year over year to $66.12. The recovery is real in both cities. It is not the same recovery.

The debt underneath the recovery

The optimistic reading has a balance sheet attached to it. Trepp reports the office CMBS delinquency rate at 12.2% in September, and non-performing matured balloon loans made up 81% of newly delinquent balances in August. CRED iQ counts about $39 billion of office CMBS maturing in the next twelve months. The Mortgage Bankers Association reported that 4.97% of CMBS loan balances were 30 or more days delinquent in the third quarter, up from 4.82%. The buildings that cannot refinance are not the prime towers with waiting lists. They are the commodity buildings in the 18.2% average, and their owners are the ones offering the concession packages.

CBRE itself adds a wrinkle. In its third-quarter earnings report, the firm said its office leasing revenue fell 2% from a year earlier, and its chief executive blamed supply rather than weak demand: the best buildings are full, and there is nothing left to lease. CBRE expects office investment volume to rise 10% this year to $91.1 billion, and expects vacancy to keep falling, to roughly 14% by the early 2030s. It also notes that market asking rents are still about 40% below the level needed to justify new construction, which suggests rent growth can continue, because nobody is building the competition.

The counterargument: the data disagrees with itself

The recovery story deserves its skepticism, because the data disagrees about the direction. Hubble’s quarterly report, using Yardi Research data, puts national vacancy at 17.8% and says national asking rents for traditional office space fell 1.5% to $33.18 per square foot, while CBRE reports rents rising 3.1% to $37.95. Both cannot be right about the direction of rents, and the difference is methodology: different samples, different definitions of asking rent, different treatment of concessions. The concession arms race documented in this series is the reason the disagreement exists. When the headline rent is $42 and the effective rent is $23, the number you report depends on which one you measure.

There is also the question of whether the return-to-office mandates will hold. The 89% figure is a survey of employer policy, not of employee attendance, and the gap between the two is where the next leasing cycle will be decided. If attendance lags the mandate, the next round of renewals will shrink footprints again. And the AI question, which CBRE dismisses as a near-term demand risk, is the one that keeps occupiers awake: if artificial intelligence cuts white-collar headcount, office demand falls regardless of the mandate.

But the counterargument has a limit. Leasing volume on pace for a record, ten straight quarters of positive absorption, and a 250-basis-point collapse in prime vacancy are not survey artifacts. They are signed leases. The market that spent five years shrinking has spent ten quarters growing, and the growth is concentrated exactly where the mandates are strictest and the buildings are best.

What to do with a split market

For tenants, the implication is timing. Prime space in the best submarkets is now a scarce good with a waiting list, and the concession packages that defined the last three years do not apply there. A tenant who needs prime space in 2027 should be negotiating now, while the flight to quality is still a trend rather than a panic. A tenant who needs commodity space has the opposite leverage: the landlord of a 26%-vacant tower will pay for the privilege of a ten-year signature, and the package, not the rent, is where the money is.

For landlords, the implication is honesty about which market they are in. A prime landlord who offers concessions is leaving money on the table. A commodity landlord who holds the face rent and refuses the package is leaving the building empty. The 18.2% national vacancy rate describes neither of them. It describes the average of two markets that have stopped behaving like one.

And for everyone watching the number, the lesson of 2026 is that the headline is the least informative figure in the report. The vacancy rate fell. The asking rent rose. The effective rent, the delinquency rate, and the waiting list tell you which of those two facts matters for your building. In a market that split in two, the sophisticated tenant prices the package, the sophisticated landlord prices the scarcity, and the naive one negotiates the average.
