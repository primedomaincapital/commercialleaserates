What Do Warehouse Lease Rates Cost Per Square Foot in 2026?

The national average warehouse rent is $11.16 a square foot, but the building you actually want costs $17 — or $27. Power, clear height, and dock doors now decide the rent more than the zip code does.

By Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

In June 2026, a third-party logistics operator toured two warehouses ten miles apart in California’s Inland Empire. The first was a 1980s box: 24-foot ceilings, one dock door for every 10,000 square feet, and electrical service installed before conveyors and robotics existed. The second was built in 2024: 36-foot clear height, a dock door every 5,000 square feet, and 4,000-amp power with capacity for EV charging. The newer building asked nearly twice the rent per square foot. The operator signed for the newer one — and told its broker the expensive building was the cheaper one to run. That decision, repeated thousands of times this year, is why the national average warehouse rent tells you almost nothing about what your warehouse will cost.

The average itself is straightforward. CBRE’s third-quarter 2026 report puts the national average net asking rent for industrial space at $11.16 per square foot, up 3.6% from a year earlier, with vacancy at 6.4%. Leasing reached 276.8 million square feet in the quarter, up 27.1% year over year, and 842.3 million square feet for the first nine months, up 17.9%. Net absorption — the change in occupied space — totaled 104.6 million square feet, the highest quarterly figure since the fourth quarter of 2022. Leases of 700,000 square feet or more nearly doubled. Cushman & Wakefield’s competing report tells a similar story at slightly different levels: $10.40 per square foot nationally, vacancy at 6.8%, rent growth of 4.0% year over year, and 76.6 million square feet of absorption, the most since the first quarter of 2023. Demand, by either count, has come back. The argument is over the price, and the price depends on which building you mean.

The map: $6 in Greenville, $27 in New York

Start with geography, because the spread is the first thing a tenant feels. Cushman & Wakefield’s third-quarter figures put the New York City outer boroughs at $27.31 per square foot — the highest asking rent of any market the firm tracks — against $17.15 in Northern New Jersey and $15.35 in Central New Jersey. The San Francisco Peninsula asks $25.68. Los Angeles asks $15.92. The Inland Empire, the logistics basin east of Los Angeles, asks $12.17. Move inland and the numbers fall fast: Chicago asks $7.53, Atlanta $7.71, California’s Central Valley $8.46, and Greenville-Spartanburg $6.03 — a record for that market, set while 8.5% of its space sat empty. Dallas-Fort Worth asks $9.76, up 19.9% in a year, the sharpest increase among major markets. A tenant choosing between a coastal gateway and an inland hub is not choosing between two prices for the same product. The gateway rent buys port access, same-day delivery to 20 million people, and a labor pool. The inland rent buys room.

One more number belongs in any honest answer: the quoted rent is not the occupancy cost. Industrial rents are usually quoted net, on a triple-net basis, which means property taxes, insurance, and common-area maintenance sit on top of the base rent. Broker-published guides put those charges at roughly $2 to $5 per square foot a year, depending on the market and the building — so a $10.40 national average is closer to $13 or $14 all-in before a tenant spends a dollar on its own operations. Office rents, by contrast, are often quoted gross, with some or all of those charges built in. Comparing the two markets on headline rent alone is a category error, and it is the most common one in this conversation.

The spec premium: power, height, and doors

Geography sets the range. The building’s specifications set the price inside it — and the gap between a modern building and an old one is now the widest it has been in the cycle. The clearest measure is height. A 36-foot-clear building stacks roughly 30% more pallets than a 28-foot building on the same footprint, which is why tenants increasingly price space per pallet position rather than per square foot. The modern gateway standard runs 32 to 40 feet clear, with a dock door every 5,000 to 7,500 square feet and 2,000 to 4,000 amps of power. The legacy standard is 24 to 28 feet, a door every 10,000 square feet, and 1,200 amps. The old building is not a cheaper version of the new one. For a tenant running automation, it is a building that cannot do the job at any price.

The absorption data proves the sorting is real. In Houston, properties built since 2023 absorbed 9.8 million square feet over the past year, while properties built before 2000 recorded negative absorption of 1.6 million square feet — tenants left the old buildings faster than new ones moved in. Nationally, Cushman & Wakefield counts 137 million square feet absorbed so far in 2026 by warehouses built since 2020. JLL’s second-quarter report made the same point in its own language: tenants prioritized power availability, automation-ready specifications, and skilled labor access over discounted rents in older facilities. In Dallas-Fort Worth, Class A-plus properties in the best locations command rents well above the $9.56 market average, while Class B and C buildings are offering concessions just to stay occupied — and one Texas analysis expects negative effective rent changes for Class B and C space through December 2026.

Power is the constraint that turns a premium into a wall. Heavy power is expensive and slow to add after construction, so buildings that already carry ample electrical service command a premium from automated and refrigerated users who would otherwise face long utility lead times. Cold storage is the extreme case: CBRE estimates it costs two to three times as much to build as dry warehouse space, and the refrigerated dock, insulation, and life-safety systems behind it mean cold rents run far above dry rents for the same square footage. A tenant that needs power should treat it as a pass-fail test at the first tour, not a negotiation point at the LOI — by the time the lease is drafted, the utility’s queue has already set the move-in date.

The counterargument: the brokers can’t agree on the price

None of this should be read as precision. The brokerages disagree with each other in ways that matter. In Dallas-Fort Worth, Cushman & Wakefield puts vacancy at 7.3% and CBRE at 7.8%. Kidder Mathews put average direct asking rent at $9.34 in the second quarter; by the third, Matthews had it at a record $10.36 and CBRE at about $10.39. Nationally, CBRE’s $11.16 and Cushman & Wakefield’s $10.40 differ by 7% — enough to change a 200,000-square-foot tenant’s annual rent by more than $150,000. The firms track different building sets, define asking rent differently, and treat concessions differently. Neither is wrong. Comparing one firm’s number to another firm’s number from a different quarter is.

The market also has a glut problem that the averages hide. Austin’s industrial vacancy sits at 21.8% with asking rent at $11.96, after years of speculative buildings delivering empty. And the newest buildings carry the highest vacancy of any age group: Cushman & Wakefield’s first-quarter report put vacancy in newly built space at 8.7%, against 5.6% for older product — the new buildings are absorbing the most space and still sitting the emptiest, because so much of them was built at once. The 30% to 50% pricing gap between modern Class A and older Class B facilities that industry guides cite is an estimate from trade coverage, not a broker print, and it should be read as one. The honest version of the 2026 warehouse market is not a single number. It is a sorting process with a price attached to each tier — and the tiers are moving in opposite directions.

What the number means for your lease

For tenants, the implication is to price the operation, not the quote. A 36-foot building at $12 that holds 30% more pallets beats a 24-foot building at $9 that cannot hold the inventory. A building with 4,000-amp service and a dock door every 5,000 square feet beats a cheaper box that needs a year of utility work and a trailer court that cannot turn a 53-foot trailer. Get the power letter and the dock count in writing before the LOI, and budget the $2 to $5 of triple-net charges on top of every quoted rent — the number that matters is the all-in occupancy cost, and nobody quotes it first.

For landlords, the implication is the mirror image. A modern building with power and height can hold its rent in a 6.4% vacancy market; an older building without them is competing on concessions, and the concession is the rent. The capital question is whether a spec upgrade — power, doors, clear height where the structure allows — costs less than the vacancy it prevents. For the pre-2000 buildings recording negative absorption in Houston, the answer is increasingly yes, and the owners who refuse the math are the ones funding their tenants’ moving costs with free rent.

And for everyone watching the average, the lesson of 2026 is that $11.16 describes a market that no longer exists as a single market. The warehouse business split into two — buildings that can run a modern supply chain and buildings that cannot — and the rent now follows the specification sheet more closely than the map. The sophisticated tenant prices the pallet position. The sophisticated landlord prices the power. The naive one negotiates the average.
