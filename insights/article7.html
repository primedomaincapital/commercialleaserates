How Long Does It Take to Negotiate a Commercial Lease?

Most commercial lease negotiations take three to nine months — but that average hides a one-month retail deal and a two-year headquarters build-out. The calendar is set by five clauses and one construction schedule.

By Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

A dental practice in Austin signed its letter of intent in February 2026 and planned to open in June. It opened in November. Nothing went wrong, exactly. The landlord’s attorney took three weeks to produce a first draft. The personal guarantee took two rounds of negotiation. The build-out permit took six weeks. The contractor found a plumbing problem in week eight. Every stage behaved normally, and the normal stages added up to nine months. That is the first thing to understand about commercial lease negotiations: the timeline is not set by any single delay. It is set by the number of stages, and by the fact that they run one after another.

The headline range is wide enough to be useless without the detail. Broker guides put most commercial lease negotiations at three to nine months from first serious conversation to signed lease, with simple deals closing in one to four weeks and complex ones stretching past a year. Ardor CRE, a brokerage that publishes a phase-by-phase timeline, puts the letter-of-intent-to-signing stretch at three to eight weeks and the full run from signed LOI to rent commencement at three to six months — longer if the build-out is heavy. Minnis, a tenant-rep firm, calls nine to twelve months a healthy planning horizon. None of these figures comes from a survey; they are broker guides built from deal experience, and they disagree with each other at the edges. Treat them as planning bands, not promises.

The phases, with real durations

Every lease moves through the same stages. The search takes four to eight weeks of market research before an LOI, according to the phase guide published by LeaseAI. The letter of intent — the non-binding document that fixes rent, term, tenant improvement allowance, and free rent before lawyers get involved — takes one to three weeks to negotiate in Ardor’s timeline, two to three weeks in LeaseAI’s, and one to two months in Minnis’s, which counts the counteroffers. Due diligence — zoning checks, licensing, estoppel certificates — runs two to four weeks in parallel with drafting. The landlord’s attorney then drafts the lease, which takes two to four weeks, and the review and redline rounds take two to six weeks for a straightforward deal, four to eight in a more contested one. The spread between the fastest and slowest version of the same stage is the whole story: a cooperative landlord with a standard form and a tenant with pre-approved lease language can move in weeks; a landlord’s attorney working from a landlord-favorable form, a tenant’s counsel redlining every clause, and a lender reviewing the lease can take months.

The property type sets the calendar more than the negotiation does. LoopNet’s leasing guide, built on benchmarks from Chris Perry, a founding principal at brokerage AQUILA Commercial, says a small move-in-ready office under 10,000 square feet can close in three to six months, while a larger space needing a full shell build-out calls for starting 18 to 24 months ahead — and it advises beginning the search six to 24 months before the current lease expires, depending on size and construction needs. Retail runs longer still: Site Logic’s Florida guide, written by Alex Bord, puts the run from decision to opening at 12 to 18 months, with two to four months for the LOI and lease and six to nine months for design, permits, and build-out. Medical tenants face the same math with licensing on top, and Ardor advises starting the search 12 to 18 months ahead. The build-out itself is a separate clock: EB3 Construction’s contractor guide, cited by LoopNet, lists four to eight weeks for minor work and roughly eight to 16 weeks for partial build-outs with layout changes — before permits, which run on the city’s schedule, not the tenant’s.

The five clauses that stall a signing

The negotiation rarely stalls on rent. It stalls on five clauses, and the first is the personal guarantee. Landlords routinely require the business owner to guarantee the lease personally, which bypasses the liability shield the LLC or corporation was formed to provide. The dangerous version is unlimited, covers the full remaining term, and survives the business’s bankruptcy or dissolution — a tenant whose business fails in year three of a seven-year lease personally owes four years of rent. The negotiated versions are a cap at 12 to 24 months of rent, a burn-off after 12 to 24 months of on-time payments, or a “good guy” guaranty that extinguishes the liability once the tenant vacates and surrenders the space properly. Every one of those structures is a separate negotiation, and each one can consume a round of redlines.

The second is assignment and subletting — the exit clause. A lease that requires landlord consent for any assignment, with no standard of reasonableness, lets the landlord refuse a buyer for the tenant’s business for any reason, or condition consent on a rent increase and a fresh guarantee from the buyer’s principals. The deal falls apart; the lease survives. Tenants should ask for consent “not to be unreasonably withheld, conditioned, or delayed,” carve out permitted transfers to affiliates and to a buyer of all or substantially all of the business, and strike recapture rights that let the landlord take the space back instead of approving a transfer. Watch, too, for profit-sharing on sublease income and continuing liability that keeps the original tenant on the hook after a successful assignment. In Massachusetts, courts generally do not read a reasonableness standard into a commercial lease unless the document says so — the protection exists only if it is written down.

The third is common-area maintenance. CAM charges with no cap and no audit right are, in the judgment of several tenant-side lawyers, the most expensive clause tenants overlook. The fix is a cap on annual increases, an audit right, and — before signing — the last two years of actual CAM reconciliations rather than the landlord’s pro forma. The fourth is the end of the lease: restoration obligations that require the tenant to return the space to its original condition, and holdover penalties that run to 200% of the final rent, month to month, terminable on 30 days’ notice. A tenant 45 days late finding new space pays two months at double rate and can be forced out during its most vulnerable moment; the negotiated version is 110% to 125% for the first 60 days, escalating thereafter, with at least 60 days’ notice before termination. The fifth, for retail, is co-tenancy and exclusivity: if the anchor tenant leaves, foot traffic falls while the rent stays fixed, unless the lease says otherwise — and renewal option windows that require nine to twelve months’ notice, which, missed, forfeit the option entirely.

The counterargument: fast deals exist, and slow is not always the tenant’s fault

The case against planning for nine months is that plenty of deals close in weeks. A simple lease in a soft market, a landlord with a standard form, a tenant with a strong balance sheet — one to four weeks is real, and brokers who quote three to nine months as a rule are describing the middle of the distribution, not the edge. There is also a cost to moving slowly: a tenant that spends four months negotiating a guarantee in a tight submarket can lose the space to a tenant that signed in two weeks. Pre-approving standard lease language before the LOI stage shortens the process considerably, and the fastest deals are usually the ones where both sides agreed the business terms before the lawyers met the document.

But the fast deal is the exception that proves the calendar, and the slow deal is not always the tenant’s doing. A landlord’s attorney who takes three weeks to produce a first draft, a lender that must approve the lease form, a co-tenancy clause that requires the anchor’s consent — none of these are the tenant stalling. They are the structure of the transaction, and they are invisible in the headline range. The honest answer to “how long does it take” is that the negotiation takes as long as the slowest party’s review cycle, and the slowest party is usually the one with the least incentive to hurry.

What to do with the calendar

Start nine to twelve months before the space is needed, and start the search before the current lease’s renewal window opens — renewal options often require six to twelve months’ notice, and missing one forfeits the option. Negotiate the guarantee and the assignment clause at the LOI stage, when goodwill is high and nothing has been spent on drafting; a clause settled in the LOI rarely reopens in the lease. Run the parallel tracks in parallel: entity formation, bank accounts, and guarantee arrangements while the lease is being drafted, not after. And calendar every date the day the lease is signed — the renewal deadline, the option window, the holdover trigger — because the clause that costs the most in year five is the one nobody remembered in year one.

The negotiation is not a single event with a single duration. It is a sequence of stages, each with its own clock, and five clauses that can each add a month. The tenants who move in on schedule are not the ones who negotiated hardest. They are the ones who started earliest, settled the exit clauses first, and treated the calendar as part of the deal — because it is.
