Lease Renewal vs. Relocation: What the 2026 Numbers Say Tenants Should Do

An Edmonton landlord offered a renewing tenant $16.00 a square foot and a new tenant $9.40 — for the same space. The renewal is the cheapest lease a landlord ever signs. The 2026 numbers on when to stay and when to move.

By Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

An Edmonton tenant with a lease expiring in 2026 received two numbers from the same landlord. Renew in place: four months of free rent, $5 a square foot of refresh allowance, and a net effective rent of $16.00. Move out and let a new tenant take the space: the landlord would offer the new tenant $30 a square foot of tenant improvement allowance, and the net effective rent would be $9.40. The same space, the same landlord, the same year — and the renewal was worth $6.60 a square foot more to the landlord than a new lease. The tenant who renews is not the tenant who gets the worse deal. The tenant who renews is the tenant the landlord can least afford to lose, and the 2026 numbers say the tenant should price that fact into the negotiation.

The market has moved in the renewing tenant's favor, and it has moved fast. CBRE's second-quarter 2026 figures put national office vacancy at 18.3%, down 30 basis points in a single quarter — the steepest single-quarter decline in the series. Renewals were signed an average of 219 days before expiry in 2025, nearly a month sooner than in 2024, as tenants moved early to lock in terms before the market tightened further. The message of the numbers is that the renewal is no longer the default for a tenant who could not be bothered to move. It is a negotiation with its own economics, and in 2026 the economics usually favor staying.

What relocation actually costs

The case for moving is usually made on the rent. A tenant in a building at $40 a square foot sees a building across the street at $34 and assumes the move saves $6. It does not, because the rent is not the cost. CBRE's total-cost-of-occupancy analysis puts non-base-rent costs — tenant improvements, free rent, operating expenses, and the rest — at 31.4% of the total cost of occupancy. The base rent is less than 70% of what the tenant pays. A move that cuts the base rent by 15% and adds a full tenant improvement build-out, months of downtime, and a new furniture and IT fit-out can easily cost more than staying at the higher rent.

The tenant improvement allowance is the largest single item, and it is where the renewal's advantage is widest. Class A gateway tenant improvement allowances run $70 to $90 a square foot in 2026 — 75% above pre-pandemic levels — and trophy Manhattan space, which peaked at $212 a square foot in 2024, has pulled back to $133 to $162. In Scottsdale, Class A allowances run $40 to $80 a square foot, with three to six months of free rent. In Edmonton, Cresa puts the build-out cost at $105 to $120 a square foot. The landlord who offers $30 a square foot to a new tenant is not being generous. The landlord is paying for the privilege of filling a vacancy, and the cost of that privilege is the difference between the renewal's net effective rent and the new tenant's — $6.60 a square foot in the Edmonton example, every year, for the life of the lease.

Free rent is the second item, and it cuts the same way. A ten-year gateway lease carries eight to twelve months of free rent in 2026, with peaks of 14.5 months in Midtown Manhattan. A renewal carries four months. The new tenant gets two to three times the free rent, and the landlord's net effective rent falls accordingly — which is why the face-to-effective rent discount on new leases runs 20% to 35%. The discount is not a gift. It is the landlord's cost of vacancy, and the renewing tenant should ask for a share of it.

When relocation wins anyway

The case for staying is strong, but it is not universal, and the exceptions matter. The first is fit: a tenant whose headcount has grown 40% since the lease was signed cannot renew into a space that no longer holds its people, however cheap the renewal. The second is the building itself: a tenant in a building with failing systems, a landlord in financial distress, or a submarket that has emptied out is not saving money by staying. The third is the market: in a submarket where vacancy is still rising and new supply is coming online, the new-tenant concessions are at their widest, and the tenant who moves captures them.

The numbers support the exceptions. CBRE's analysis shows that tenant improvement allowances reduce total cost of occupancy by 8% to 15%, and free rent by 3% to 8% — which means that for a tenant who genuinely needs a new build-out, the move's concessions are worth 11% to 23% of the total cost. A tenant who needs $100 a square foot of work done is better off moving to a building where the landlord pays for it than staying in a building where the tenant pays for it. The renewal wins when the space still works. The relocation wins when it does not.

The counterargument: the landlord knows the renewal is cheaper, and prices it accordingly

The case against the renewal argument is that the landlord has read the same numbers. A landlord who knows the renewal saves $6.60 a square foot does not give the tenant $6.60. The landlord gives the tenant enough to keep them — four months of free rent instead of twelve, $5 of refresh instead of $30 of build-out — and keeps the difference. The tenant who renews without a credible threat to move gets the landlord's opening offer, which is the cheapest retention the landlord can buy. The renewal's economics only become the tenant's economics if the tenant is willing to leave.

That argument is right, and it is the reason the renewal negotiation should start with a market tour. The tenant who has toured three competing buildings, has a term sheet from one of them, and can show the landlord the number the landlord would have to beat to keep the tenant is the tenant who captures the renewal's value. The tenant who renews without looking is the tenant who pays the landlord's price for the privilege of staying. The 219-day average — renewals signed more than seven months before expiry — is the market's answer to that problem: tenants are starting early, touring early, and negotiating from a position of knowledge rather than urgency.

For a tenant, the practical conclusion is to price both options on total cost of occupancy, not on base rent — to add the tenant improvement allowance, the free rent, the downtime, and the fit-out to the relocation's side of the ledger, and to compare the net effective rent of the renewal against the net effective rent of the move. For a landlord, it is to recognize that the renewal is the cheapest lease it will ever sign, and to price the retention accordingly rather than testing how little the tenant will accept. And for anyone in the business of lease rate data, the missing number is now obvious. Nobody publishes the renewal-versus-relocation net effective rent spread by market, by building class, over time. The firm that does will own the next argument in commercial leasing.
